# The Day We Found the Universe — Visual Notes

> **Book:** Marcia Bartusiak, *The Day We Found the Universe*  
> **Purpose of this page:** a compact visual memory aid plus detailed notes on the people, dates, observations, and conceptual steps that transformed the Milky Way from “the universe” into one galaxy among many.

The central lesson is that the discovery was **not a single observation by a single person**. It was a chain of measurements:

**Leavitt → a distance ruler · Slipher → velocities · Shapley/Curtis → competing cosmic models · Hubble → decisive extragalactic distances · Lemaître/Hubble → expansion**

### The chain to remember

| Step | Person(s) | What became measurable? | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Standard candle** | Henrietta Leavitt | Intrinsic luminosity from Cepheid period | Makes large astronomical distances measurable |
| **2. Calibration** | Ejnar Hertzsprung and others | Absolute scale for Cepheids | Converts the empirical relation into a distance tool |
| **3. Galactic scale** | Harlow Shapley | Distribution of globular clusters and variable stars | Shows the Milky Way is large and the Sun is not central |
| **4. Velocities** | Vesto Slipher | Radial velocity of spiral nebulae | Reveals motions far larger than ordinary nearby objects |
| **5. Competing models** | Shapley & Heber Curtis | Logical consequences of available evidence | Frames the “one galaxy vs many galaxies” question |
| **6. Decisive distance** | Edwin Hubble | Distance to Andromeda | Establishes that Andromeda lies outside the Milky Way |
| **7. Expansion** | Georges Lemaître, Edwin Hubble, Slipher's velocity data | Relation between distance and recession | Changes cosmology from static architecture to dynamical evolution |

## Why distance was the key problem

Before the 1920s, astronomers could photograph fuzzy **spiral nebulae**, but appearance alone could not establish whether they were relatively nearby structures inside the Milky Way or immense stellar systems far beyond it. The decisive quantity was **distance**.

**Stellar parallax** gives a geometric distance, but early twentieth-century telescopes could use it only for relatively nearby stars. Extragalactic astronomy therefore required a **standard candle**: an object whose intrinsic luminosity could be estimated from an observable property.

## Henrietta Swan Leavitt — 1908–1912

While studying photographic plates of variable stars in the Magellanic Clouds at Harvard, **Henrietta Swan Leavitt** recognized that Cepheid variables obey a systematic relation:

> **Longer pulsation period → greater intrinsic luminosity.**

The clever part is the geometry. Stars in a given Magellanic Cloud are approximately at the same distance compared with the much smaller dimensions of the Cloud itself. Therefore, differences in apparent brightness among those Cepheids mostly reflect differences in **intrinsic luminosity**, allowing the period–luminosity pattern to emerge.

Once the relation is calibrated, the logic is:

1. Observe the Cepheid's pulsation period.
2. Infer its intrinsic luminosity from the period–luminosity relation.
3. Measure its apparent brightness.
4. Compare intrinsic and apparent brightness.
5. Infer distance through the inverse-square law.

This is one of the hidden foundations of the entire story. Hubble's later distance to Andromeda depended on the existence of this ruler.

## Ejnar Hertzsprung — 1913

**Ejnar Hertzsprung** made an early attempt to calibrate Leavitt's relation. Leavitt had established the *relative* relation; calibration was required to attach an absolute luminosity scale and thereby turn it into a quantitative distance method.

The calibration was not yet modern and later required important revisions, but it moved Cepheids from a pattern on photographic plates toward a genuine **cosmic yardstick**.

## Harlow Shapley — 1917–1918

**Harlow Shapley** used variable stars and the spatial distribution of globular clusters to estimate the architecture of the Milky Way.

His important conclusions were:

- The Milky Way is much larger than many astronomers had previously assumed.
- The Sun is **not** near the center.
- The globular-cluster system points toward a galactic center in the direction of Sagittarius.

Shapley therefore helped remove the Sun from a privileged cosmic location.

His scale was imperfect. Interstellar absorption, variable-star calibration, and the effects of dust were not fully understood, so he overestimated the Galaxy's physical dimensions. But the qualitative revolution was real: **our Solar System was not central even within our own Galaxy.**

### Memory hook

**Leavitt supplied the ruler. Hertzsprung helped calibrate it. Shapley used distance indicators to redraw the Milky Way. Hubble then used the same family of ideas to move beyond the Milky Way.**

## What were the spiral nebulae?

The spiral nebulae were faint, often beautifully structured objects seen through telescopes. Their status was one of astronomy's deepest unresolved questions.

Two possibilities existed:

- **Local interpretation:** the spirals are relatively small structures inside the Milky Way.
- **Island-universe interpretation:** the spirals are enormous stellar systems at distances far beyond the Milky Way.

The difference was existential. If the first interpretation were true, our Galaxy might constitute nearly the entire universe. If the second were true, the Milky Way would become one system among an enormous population of galaxies.

## Vesto Melvin Slipher — 1912–1917

At Lowell Observatory, **Vesto Melvin Slipher** measured spectra of spiral nebulae. Spectral-line shifts gave their radial velocities through the Doppler effect.

His observations were startling:

- Many spirals had very large radial velocities.
- A large fraction were **redshifted**, indicating recession.
- Andromeda was an important exception: it was blueshifted because its local motion is toward the Milky Way.

These measurements did **not by themselves give distances**, so they could not alone prove that the objects were galaxies. But they made the spiral nebulae look dynamically unlike ordinary nearby clouds.

Slipher's velocity measurements later became essential raw material for the distance–velocity relation associated with the expanding universe.

## Adriaan van Maanen — the misleading evidence

**Adriaan van Maanen** reported measurements of apparent internal rotation in spiral nebulae.

The implication seemed powerful: if a spiral were millions of light-years away and nevertheless showed visible angular rotation over only a few years, its physical rotation speed would have to be absurdly large — potentially exceeding physically reasonable limits. Therefore, if van Maanen's measurements were correct, the spirals had to be nearby.

They were not correct.

The episode is important because it illustrates how science actually develops: a technically respected measurement can be wrong, yet still influence theory and debate for years. The astronomy community had to reconcile contradictory data before the extragalactic interpretation became secure.

## 1920 — the Shapley–Curtis Great Debate

The famous discussion between **Harlow Shapley** and **Heber Curtis** is often summarized as a contest over the size of the universe, but the historical reality is more interesting.

### Shapley was right about

- The Milky Way being much larger than older models suggested.
- The Sun being far from the Galactic center.
- The older Sun-centered picture of the Galaxy being untenable.

### Curtis was right about

- Spiral nebulae being independent stellar systems.
- The existence of galaxies outside the Milky Way.
- The qualitative “island universe” interpretation.

### Why the debate did not settle the issue

Neither side possessed the decisive direct distance measurement. Arguments from brightness, novae, structure, velocities, and apparent rotations could be interpreted in competing ways.

The debate therefore represents an **intellectual cliffhanger**, not a final verdict.

### Memory hook

**Slipher supplied velocities. Shapley and Curtis supplied competing interpretations. van Maanen supplied misleading apparent motions. Hubble later supplied the decisive distance.**

## 1923 — the decisive Andromeda observation

Using the **100-inch Hooker Telescope** at Mount Wilson Observatory, **Edwin Hubble** photographed the Andromeda nebula and identified a variable star that proved to be a Cepheid.

That changed the problem completely.

A spiral nebula's appearance could be debated. A Cepheid supplied a calibrated distance indicator. Once Hubble estimated Andromeda's distance, the number was far too large for Andromeda to fit inside even Shapley's enlarged Milky Way.

Hubble's original numerical distance was smaller than the modern value because Cepheid populations and calibration were not yet fully understood. Nevertheless, the logical conclusion was robust:

> **Andromeda is outside the Milky Way.**

That observation transformed “spiral nebulae” into **galaxies**.

## January 1, 1925 — the public turning point

Hubble's findings were presented at the American Astronomical Society meeting at the beginning of 1925. This public announcement supplies the symbolic “day” in Bartusiak's title.

The conceptual universe changed from:

**one dominant stellar system**  
to  
**a universe populated by many galaxies.**

The modern distance to Andromeda is roughly **2.5 million light-years**. Hubble did not have the modern value, but he did not need it: his result only had to show that Andromeda lay far beyond the dimensions of the Milky Way.

## Why Hubble's early distance was imperfect

Several calibration problems remained:

- Cepheid variable stars exist in different populations with different luminosity calibrations.
- Interstellar and internal dust attenuate apparent brightness.
- Early photographic measurements had limited sensitivity and calibration accuracy.
- The cosmic distance ladder was still being constructed.

These issues changed the **number**, but not the **conclusion** that Andromeda was extragalactic.

## 1927 — Georges Lemaître

**Georges Lemaître**, working from general relativity, developed a dynamical cosmological solution in which the universe expands. He also connected the theoretical expansion with available astronomical velocity and distance information.

Lemaître's role is essential because the expanding universe was not merely an empirical straight line drawn through observations. A relativistic theory already existed that gave the distance–recession relation a physical cosmological meaning.

## 1929 — Hubble's distance–velocity relation

Hubble published a relation between estimated galaxy distances and recession velocities. Much of the radial-velocity information had originated in Slipher's spectroscopy.

The emerging empirical pattern was:

[
v \propto d
]

or in the familiar modern form,

[
v = H_0 d.
]

For nearby galaxies, this relation expresses the approximately linear recession expected from cosmic expansion.

### Important historical nuance

The simplified phrase “Hubble discovered the expanding universe” hides a multi-person chain:

- **Slipher** measured many of the crucial velocities.
- **Leavitt** supplied the foundation of the Cepheid distance scale.
- **Lemaître** derived and interpreted an expanding relativistic universe before Hubble's 1929 paper.
- **Hubble** established extragalactic distances and published the influential observational distance–velocity relation.

## The conceptual revolution

By the end of this sequence:

1. The Sun was no longer at the center of the Milky Way.
2. The Milky Way was no longer the whole universe.
3. Spiral nebulae became independent galaxies.
4. The universe itself became **dynamic rather than static**.
5. Distance and velocity became linked on cosmological scales.

# Final memory compression

> **Leavitt made distance measurable. Slipher measured motion. Shapley and Curtis argued over the architecture. Hubble proved Andromeda was another galaxy. Lemaître and Hubble helped establish that the galaxy-filled universe is expanding.**

The most useful way to remember Bartusiak's story is as a sequence of **instrument + observable + inference**:

**photographic plates → variable-star periods → luminosities → distances → galactic scale → spectra → velocities → external galaxies → cosmic expansion.**
